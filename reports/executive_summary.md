# Soft drinks: price, promotion and forecasting -- executive summary

**For:** VP Sales / Revenue Management  **Data:** 93 Dominick's supermarkets in Chicago,
Sep 1989 - May 1997, weekly sales of every soft drink (17.7M store x product x week records).
**Brands analysed in depth:** Coke, Diet Coke, Pepsi, Diet Pepsi, 7Up and the store's own label,
in 2-litre bottles and 24-pack cans.

---

## The five things to know

**1. Shoppers are very sensitive to soft-drink prices -- most of all on 24-packs.**
A 10% price cut on a 24-pack raises its volume by roughly 63-92%; on a 2-litre bottle by roughly
36-53%. (Technically: own-price elasticities of -4.6 to -6.2 for 24-packs and -2.9 to -4.0 for
2-litres. An elasticity of -5 means a 1% price cut adds about 5% volume.) 24-packs are a planned,
stock-up purchase, so shoppers wait for the deal.

**2. This category runs on promotions.**
79-94% of all units of these brands are sold on some kind of deal; core brands are on promotion in
43-66% of store-weeks, at an average discount of 20-26%. During a promotion a 24-pack sells about
16 times its normal volume, a 2-litre about 5 times.

**3. On average, promotions do not pay for themselves -- but moderate ones do.**
Across 39,000 promoted store-weeks, the extra margin from extra units covered only 89 cents of
every dollar of discount given away (a margin loss of about $2.2M on $19.6M of discount over 72
weeks). The depth of the discount decides it:

| Discount depth | Return per $1 of discount (24-pack / 2-litre) |
|---|---|
| 10-20% off | **$1.30 / $1.34 -- pays back** |
| 20-30% off | $0.90 / $1.23 |
| 30%+ off | **$0.72 / $0.82 -- loses money** |

Yet two-thirds of all discount money went to 30%+ deals. Price cuts without in-store promotion
support (no sale tag / bonus-buy) were the weakest of all: about 50 cents back per dollar.

**4. Our promotions take volume from competitors -- and vice versa.**
When one brand cuts price, shoppers switch: e.g. a 10% cut on Coke 2-litre pulls about 2-3% of the
volume of Pepsi, Diet Pepsi, 7Up and store-brand 2-litre. Part of every promotion's "lift" is
brand switching, not new category growth. After a 24-pack promotion, sales of that pack dip by
about 8-18% the next week as shoppers work through the cans they stocked up on.

**5. We can forecast store-level weekly demand much better than simple rules of thumb.**
A machine-learning forecast that knows the promotion plan cuts forecast error by 48% versus
"same week last year" (37% in promotion weeks), and by 18% versus the best traditional statistical
model. The single biggest driver of next week's volume is the planned discount.

## Recommendation

1. **Re-balance trade spend from deep to moderate discounts.** Move 30%+ deals towards 10-20%
   (e.g. $6.99-$7.49 instead of $5.89 on a 24-pack with an ~$8.40 shelf price). The data say this keeps most of the volume and
   turns a margin loss into a margin gain. Start with a test in a subset of stores (see below).
2. **Never run a price cut without in-store support.** Untagged cuts lift volume far less and
   return about half of what they cost; pair every cut with a tag, display or feature.
3. **Share the promotion calendar with demand planning.** Forecasts that know the plan are 37%
   more accurate in promotion weeks -- fewer out-of-stocks on deal, less excess stock afterwards.
4. **Plan the summer around July 4th, Labor Day and Memorial Day** -- the category's three biggest
   weeks (+27% to +37% above an average week) -- and use the scenario tool to size each event.

## Expected impact

In the 72-week period analysed, promotions with 30%+ discounts cost about $3.2M more in margin
than they generated (24-pack and 2-litre combined), on about $13M of discount. Had that money
earned what the 10-20% deals earned (about $1.30 back per $1), it would have added margin instead
of losing it. How much of that is real depends on how much volume shallower deals keep, which is
why we recommend a test in a subset of stores before rolling out.

## Caveats (please read)

- The data are from **one Chicago retailer in the 1990s**. The *methods* transfer directly to
  today's data; the specific numbers should be re-estimated before acting.
- The margin view is the **retailer's category gross margin**. It does not count extra store
  traffic or basket spend that soft-drink deals bring, or the bottler's own trade terms.
- Price sensitivity is measured mostly from **short promotions**; shoppers react less to a
  permanent price change than to a one-week deal (they stock up on deals). Treat the scenario and
  optimal-price tools as **directional** -- they also assume competitors do not react.
- Promotion records in this data set are incomplete (the provider warns about it); we added a
  price-based rule to catch unflagged deals.
